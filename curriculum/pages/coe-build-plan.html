Why the third programme is deferred, and what deferring it does not save

Engineer Training is the diploma's own module library cut short. It shares the same computer lab, the same cells and the same trainers, so dropping it removes no equipment and saves no capital. What it frees is timetable and floor space — one classroom, one AV set, and the contended cell weeks.

It can be added in any later year without buying anything, provided the cell calendar has room. Everything in this plan is therefore sized for two cohorts of 30 — the PG Diploma and the ITI Technician.

Why 1 year for the diploma

The credit rule fixes it

A Postgraduate Diploma is 40 credits. The national rule is 1 credit = 30 hours. 40 × 30 = 1,200 hours = one year. There is no shorter version that is still a PG Diploma. Loyola is autonomous under UGC, so the one-year form is open to us — the two-year rule applies only to AICTE engineering colleges.

Why 8 months for the technician

It is what the content costs

A national one-year ITI trade is 1,200 taught hours. This is 1,215 — one full trade-year, delivered in eight months at 38 hours a week. The legal ceiling for ITI training is 40 hours a week, so we are inside it. Four robot brands alone take 140 hours; the 48-fault library takes 90.

A trainee who leaves at week 16 leaves with a certificate, not with nothing. Each half is also separately fundable under short-term skilling schemes; a single 1,215-hour block would not be.

The sizing rule that keeps five tracks affordable

The diploma is 890 hours everyone takes together, then 320 hours in one of five tracks. So the foundation kit is bought 1 : 1 or 1 : 2 for all 30 students — that is the computer lab and the bench lab, and it is the bulk of the spend.

The specialisation kit only ever faces about 6 to 8 students, because 30 students split five ways. Two learning arms serve the whole Robot Learning track. One AMR serves the whole Autonomy track. Adding four tracks to the flagship does not multiply the bill — it adds one small block per track, and two of the five tracks need no new hardware at all.

The one thing worth noticing in this list

The four robot brands are not four extra robots. Cells 1 to 4 are FANUC, ABB, KUKA and Universal Robots — the same five cells, bought from four vendors instead of one. Multi-brand coverage is the thing no manufacturer's own training centre can offer, and it costs us nothing extra.

The humanoid — what it is for, stated plainly

Decision taken: one humanoid at launch. This note records what it buys, so that nobody later mistakes it for something it was not.

It carries no teaching hours. Every module in both programmes was checked. The closest is the Vision-Language-Action module, which names NVIDIA's GR00T humanoid model — but it runs in simulation and its capstone is explicitly a skill on a real arm. The humanoid does not displace the learning arms or the teleoperation rig, which do carry hours.

What it does buy: a Centre of Excellence gets visited, inspected and photographed. A humanoid is the most effective thing on the floor for that, and it is the platform ready in the building on the day a customer or the State asks about humanoid work. It should be positioned as a research and demonstration platform, not as something graduates are trained on.

One thing to hold the line on: the placement claims. Frontier humanoid firms hire globally and mostly ask for a master's with published research. Owning a humanoid does not change that, and the jobs section already says so. Keep those two facts apart in every conversation.

It also needs floor: a clear, matted demonstration area. A humanoid that falls is a real hazard and it is not covered by the cells' guarding — the bench lab grew 20 m² to hold it.

We do not buy 30 of anything. Three rotation patterns do the work.

Where the space went, against the earlier figure of 1,275 m²

Down 65 m² — one classroom, from dropping the third cohort. Up 80 m² in the cell hall — the machine-tending interface, the SCARA and delta lines, and an aisle the AMR can actually run in. Up 60 m² in the bench lab — the two track kits and a matted area for the humanoid.

Net +91 m². The 70 PPE sets now comfortably cover 60 trainees, 7 staff and visitors — at three cohorts they did not.

The cell hall decides which buildings are even candidates

Square metres are the easy part. The hall also needs 4.5 m clear height, three-phase power with proper earthing, compressed air at 7 bar piped to every cell, floor loading rated for robot pedestals, forklift or hoist access to install and move cells, fume extraction at the welding cell, and 500 lux at the benches.

An ordinary first-floor office shell cannot take a robot cell. This list should be the screening test for any building we are offered.

What must stay together

The technical block

  • The live cell hall
  • The controls and maintenance workshop
  • The robotics bench lab
  • The store and spares

These are the shared, expensive assets. Splitting them is what creates the ₹3.2–5.3 crore of duplication.

What can sit elsewhere

The academic front-end

  • Classroom teaching
  • Examinations and student records
  • Library and student services
  • Reception and administration

Keep the computer lab on the technical site unless we settle who owns, insures and holds keys to 32 GPU workstations.

On the Loyola partnership

Loyola brings the award, the admissions pipeline, examination machinery, student services and 230 hours of foundation teaching. It brings no robots, no PLCs and no safety infrastructure, and a normal college computer lab cannot run our simulation software.

So the partnership should be judged on the degree, the intake and the teaching load it absorbs — not on equipment savings, because there are none.

Two things this table shows that are worth saying out loud

Adding four specialisations costs ₹0.6–1.1 crore, not four times anything. Thirty students split five ways is about six per track, so the track kit only ever faces six people. Two of the five tracks — Factory Analytics and Agentic & Applied AI — need no new hardware at all.

The technician programme, not the diploma, absorbed the increase. Honouring the seven machine families and the assessed AMR and machine-tending hours moved that bucket from ₹0.9 cr to ₹2.1 cr at the low end. Run the technician programme alone and it now costs ₹5.3–9.0 crore.

Position the humanoid path honestly

Frontier humanoid companies — Figure, 1X, Apptronik, Tesla Optimus — hire globally and mostly ask for a PhD or a master's with published research. A diploma graduate will not be hired into them directly.

The realistic route is diploma → an Indian robotics, integrator or R&D-centre role at ₹5–8 L → three to five years of shipped work → then those companies become reachable. That version holds up with a recruiter. The direct version does not, and one graduate finding that out becomes a complaint.
